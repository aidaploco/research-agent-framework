- The discount window is an instrument of monetary policy that allows eligible institutions to borrow money from the central bank on a short‑term basis to meet temporary liquidity shortages caused by internal or external disruptions [W1]
- The interest rate charged on discount window loans is called the bank rate, discount rate, policy rate, base rate, or repo rate, and is separate from the prime rate and the federal funds rate [W1]
- In the United States the Federal Reserve offers three discount window rates: primary credit, secondary credit, and seasonal credit [W1]
- Primary credit is normally set 100 basis points above the federal funds target, secondary credit 50 basis points above primary credit, and seasonal credit is based on an average of the effective federal funds rate and 90‑day certificate of deposit rates [W1]
- Institutions borrowing from the discount window must provide acceptable collateral such as Treasury securities, municipal bonds, or mortgage loans [W1]
- The discount window was created by the Federal Reserve to help banks avoid bank runs and was originally offered at a rate below market to discourage use, creating a stigma for borrowers [W1]
- During the 2007–2009 credit crunch the Fed temporarily lowered the primary credit rate and extended loan terms to reduce the spread over the federal funds rate [W1]
- In March 2023, banks drew $153 billion from the discount window, a new record, during a wave of U.S. bank failures [W1]
